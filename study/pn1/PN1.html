  • What it is. One line in its own pane under a chart: how much of the account the deployment system has invested on each daily bar. 70% is held through pullbacks whatever the market does; the other 30% moves with the market reading, from none of it at a reading of 0 to all of it at 100.
  • It is the tool's number. The market reading is the allocation tool's own sum of its two voting parts — SPY and QQQ's RSI together, and credit's own move with rates taken out. The script carries the tool's measured tables, written into it by a program, and its arithmetic was replayed against the tool's engine.
  • It goes back to 25 Apr 2007, ten sessions after HYG began trading, and the last bar is live: it counts today's prices as they trade.
  • What to expect on TradingView. Within about a point of the tool on most days. TradingView's prices differ from the Hub's by a cent here and there, and one cent on HYG moves the reading by up to 0.45 of a point.

  • Every day there is. On all 4,894 trading days from 25 Apr 2007 to 6 Oct 2026, fed the tool's own prices, the script gives the same reading as the tool on every one.
  • With HYG's payouts added back the way TradingView does it, it is within one point on 99.8% of those days (the middle gap is 0.1; 99 days in 100 are within 0.6). The 8 days over one point all fall between 2007 and 2012; the worst is 2.2 on 28 Nov 2007. In the last year the worst gap was 0.6. The main cause is known: the tool's long table of HYG with payouts is rounded to the cent, and HYG's price in it was 31 in 2007 against 77 now, so a cent was more of it. Rounding to the cent, with nothing else changed, makes 6 such days by itself, all between 2007 and 2012.

WHAT COULD BE WRONG

  1. It has never been compiled. There is no TradingView access from here. The arithmetic is proved; the spelling of the script is not. If TradingView's editor rejects a line, Kimi may make three small named fixes, none of which touches a number, and must stop for anything else.
  2. TradingView's prices are not the Hub's. Credit's own move is a small number. On 6 Oct 2026 one cent on HYG's close moved the reading by 0.45 of a point and one cent on the Treasury fund by 0.15; a cent on SPY or QQQ moved it by 0.01. Across the last year the most a single cent moved it was 0.72. So a point of difference from the tool on an ordinary day is within what the two feeds can explain.
  3. HYG's payout day. On the first trading day of a month HYG trades without its payout (about 0.5% of its price). If TradingView were late adding it back, the reading that day would be off by about 12 points (17 at worst, in either direction) until it caught up. The tool keeps its own payout list. Worth one look on the next payout day.
  4. After the close. Until the day's close is settled, the tool goes on counting prices traded after 16:00 New York; the pane stops at the close. Today at 16:08 the tool took one stray QQQ price (741.21, against 757.73 at 16:11) and read 36 for that one read; at 16:11 it read 30 again. The pane reads the regular session only, so a price traded after the close does not reach it.
  5. Looking back flatters it. The line on a past bar is today's rule on that day's prices. The rule's curves were measured on those same years (2009 to 2026). The test on years it had not seen is on the tool's study page, not in this pane.
  6. The Treasury fund is read without its payouts — the tool's own choice, copied here. Since 2016 its close has fallen 0.19% more on its 129 payout days than on other days, which puts about +0.10 into credit's own move for the ten sessions after each one. Taking that out would have moved the reading by a middle 1.5 points (4.6 at worst) on the 121 sessions of the last year it touched. The pane and the tool agree because both do it; whether the tool should is a question for its study.
  7. The shape is typed in twice. 70 held and 30 tactical are the tool's baseline today, set as two inputs of the pane. If the shape changes in the tool, the pane must be changed by hand.

What was not done

  • Not installed, not compiled, and no picture from TradingView: that is Kimi's task, written and ready.
  • The pane draws the two voting parts only. The tool's lights — the VIX, breadth, the leaders — are not in it, and neither is the tool's switch for averaging the last few readings.
  • No alerts. Nothing deployed. No table touched.

HOW IT GETS ONTO TRADINGVIEW

  • Kimi saves it as a new script, Scintilla Deployment Pane, and builds a new layout, Scintilla — Deployment: four daily charts — SPY with the pane under it, QQQ, the VIX and HYG — set to move together, so scrolling back on one shows the same day on all four.
  • No tab that is open today is touched. The new layout is made from the app's own new-tab page.
  • Kimi then reads the pane's own numbers on eight past days and sets them beside ours, and reads back the text TradingView saved so it can be checked against the text that was proved.

DECISIONS FOR ALAN

  1. The line's colour. As built it is green on a day it has more invested and red on a day it has less — the Hub's rule for a line. The other choice is one calm teal line. Recommended: keep green and red. It is one switch in the pane's settings either way.
  2. The scale. The pane runs from 0 to 100 and the line lives between 70 and 100, so it uses the top third. That shows at a glance how much is always held. The other choice is to let the pane close in on 70 to 100. Recommended: keep 0 to 100; TradingView lets you stretch the scale by hand on the day you want the detail.
  3. Where the pane sits. Under SPY only, as asked, or under all four charts. Recommended: SPY only. It draws the same line under any symbol, so four copies add height and nothing else.
PAGE SPECS

Where each number comes from. The tool's readings: the allocation tool's engine, fed the closes its study was built from (SPY, QQQ, HYG and the 7–10 year Treasury fund from the Hub's chart service; HYG with its payouts from the study's own table). The script's readings: a replay of the script's arithmetic that reads every table and constant out of the script file. Today's rows: the tool's own live read of the chart service, and the script on the very same prices. Nothing on this page is typed in by hand; it is written by a program from the proof file.

How the drawings are made. On this page, from that replay, in the script's own colours and with its own label words. They show what the script computes. They do not show TradingView's rendering, its fonts or its prices.

The script. Paste-ready, written for version 6 of TradingView's script language. It asks TradingView for four daily series and uses 12 of the 64 plots a script may draw. No white anywhere. Its tables are written into it by a program from the tool's model file, never typed.

The colours. Green #00FFA3 and red #FF2D55 are the Hub's own up and down; teal #2FB5A8 is the family tone, used for the market reading and the shaded band. Checked with a colour-separation program on a black ground, every pair of the three: the closest pair is 11.5 apart for red-green colour blindness (the bar is 8) and 21.5 apart for full colour vision (the bar is 15), and each clears 3 to 1 against the ground. A first choice for the market reading, a brighter teal, sat only 12.6 from the green and was dropped. The green and the teal are brighter than that program's band for evenly weighted series; they are the house colours and were left as they are. A rising stretch of line is always green and a falling one always red, so the colour never carries anything the slope does not, and every line is named by a label at its last bar.

The proof and the tests. One program writes the proof file this page is built from. A set of automatic checks holds the script to the tool's model, the replay to the engine, the plot count and the no-white rule to the script's text, and this page's numbers to the proof.

Kimi's task. Written and ready beside the script. After the install, one command says whether the text TradingView saved is the text that was proved, and names every line that differs if it is not.
